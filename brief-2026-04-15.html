1. Plant-Based Diet Linked to Meaningfully Lower Dementia Risk in 93,000-Person Study

Source: Neurology; reported via CNN, EurekAlert!, Neuroscience News — 8 April 2026

What happened: A large longitudinal study of 92,849 adults, followed for an average of 11 years, found that the highest-quality plant-based diet adherence was associated with a 12% lower risk of all-cause dementia and a 7% reduction for healthful plant-based patterns specifically. Critically, the study also found that those eating the most unhealthy plant-based foods (refined grains, juices, potatoes, added sugars) had a 6% higher risk than the lowest scorers — and that participants whose diet quality deteriorated over 10 years had a 25% higher risk of dementia. Importantly, adopting a healthier diet after age 60 was still associated with reduced risk.

Why this matters to you: This is one of the largest and most ethnically diverse dietary-dementia studies to date and directly supports the scientific case for dietary intervention in your 75+ target population. The finding that late-life dietary improvement still confers benefit is particularly relevant for community-dwelling older adults who may not have had healthy diets earlier in life. The 25% elevated risk from diet deterioration is a compelling, defensible statistic for funders.

Second-order effects: The quality-not-just-quantity finding shifts the field toward precision dietary scoring rather than broad "plant-based" messaging — an opening for clinical trial design that differentiates healthy and unhealthy plant foods, which could be a distinguishing feature of your research protocols.

2. Vitamin D Levels in Midlife Predict Alzheimer's Tau Burden Decades Later

Source: ScienceDaily / University of Galway, Neurology Open Access — 7 April 2026

What happened: A study of nearly 800 participants from the Framingham Heart Study found that higher circulating vitamin D in midlife (30s–40s) was associated with significantly lower tau protein accumulation in brain scans approximately 16 years later. Tau is a primary biomarker of Alzheimer's disease. The study adds to a growing body of evidence that midlife nutritional status shapes neurodegeneration decades in advance.

Why this matters to you: For a non-profit focused on nutrition and dementia prevention in older adults, this study reinforces the case that nutritional biomarkers — not just dietary patterns — matter for Alzheimer's risk stratification. Vitamin D status is cheap to measure and commonly deficient in the 75+ population; this finding strengthens the rationale for including it as both a covariate and potential intervention target in your trials.

Second-order effects: The midlife window highlighted by this study opens a longer-horizon prevention narrative — dietary and nutritional interventions in the 50s and 60s as early Alzheimer's prevention. This could expand the scope and fundability of your mission beyond the 75+ population specifically.
