1. Target-Trial Study Links Sustained Protein Intake to Muscle Function in Adults 60+

Source: Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition — published 14 April 2026

What happened: A target-trial emulation study using NHANES data (2011–2018, 5,736 adults aged 60+) found that usual protein intake at or above 1.0 g/kg/day was associated with meaningfully better muscle performance and mobility over time. Most participants fell below this threshold. The study underscores the gap between the standard RDA of 0.8 g/kg/day and what is likely needed to preserve function in older cohorts.

Why this matters to you: This provides robust, real-world epidemiological evidence — rather than short-term supplementation data — that habitual protein shortfall is functionally consequential for the 75+ group. It strengthens the case for dietary protein as a primary intervention target in your work, not a secondary consideration.

Second-order effect: The call for randomised feeding trials to confirm causality opens a credible funding and collaboration avenue for your organisation to lead or co-lead such a trial specifically in the 75+ cohort, where effect sizes may be even larger.

2. Multivitamin Supplementation Slows Epigenetic Ageing Clocks — COSMOS Trial Ancillary Study

Source: Nature Medicine — March/April 2026

What happened: A pre-specified ancillary study of the large COSMOS randomised clinical trial found that two years of daily multivitamin-multimineral supplementation (Centrum Silver) reduced the annual rate of increase in two epigenetic ageing clocks — PCPhenoAge and PCGrimAge — by approximately 2.6 and 1.4 months respectively. Cocoa flavanol supplementation showed no significant effect on epigenetic ageing.

Why this matters to you: This is a high-quality RCT result published in a flagship journal, and it directly implicates a widely accessible, low-cost supplement in measurable biological ageing deceleration. For seniors educators and clinicians working with the 75+ population, this is a concrete, actionable finding — though the magnitude of effect and real-world functional consequences warrant careful framing.

Second-order effect: The split result — multivitamins yes, flavanols no — will likely prompt media oversimplification; your organisation is well-positioned to provide authoritative, nuanced interpretation to older adults and their carers before misinformation takes hold.

3. EAT-Lancet Diet Associated with Reduced Biological Ageing via Metabolomic Pathways

Source: npj Aging — April 2026

What happened: Using UK Biobank data, researchers compared adherence to the EAT-Lancet planetary health diet and traditional plant-based diets against biological ageing markers including telomere length and multi-organ ageing clocks. Higher EAT-Lancet adherence was more strongly associated with decreased biological ageing. Diet-specific metabolomic signatures mediated between 27% and 63% of the associations, providing a mechanistic pathway.

Why this matters to you: The EAT-Lancet diet has until now been framed primarily around planetary sustainability rather than healthy ageing. This data repositions it as a genuine longevity-relevant dietary pattern with metabolomic evidence — relevant for communicating dietary advice to older adults who may be motivated by personal health goals over environmental ones.

Second-order effect: The metabolomic mediation data opens a precision nutrition angle: identifying which specific biomarkers shift with EAT-Lancet adherence may allow targeted monitoring of dietary response in 75+ individuals.
