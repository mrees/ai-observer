2. FMT from Young Mice Prevents Liver Cancer in Aged Mice — and Reverses Core Ageing Features

Source: News-Medical.Net — Restoring Gut Microbiome May Prevent Liver Cancer in Mice (23 April 2026)

What happened: Researchers conducted a fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiment in which fecal samples from young mice were preserved and transplanted back into the same mice at an advanced age. Mice receiving the restored microbiome developed no liver cancer over the study period, while 2 of 8 aging controls did. The mechanism involves suppression of MDM2 — a gene implicated in liver cancer — which was elevated in untreated older mice and returned to youthful levels in treated animals. The study also documented reductions in inflammation, liver fibrosis, mitochondrial decline, telomere attrition, and DNA damage in the treated group. The lead researcher indicated plans to move towards first-in-human clinical trials.

Why this matters to you: This is one of the most mechanistically complete demonstrations to date that the ageing gut microbiome directly drives age-related pathology — and that a dietary/microbiome intervention can reverse it. For a nutrition science educator focused on the 75+ population, the practical message is clear: microbiome health in older adults is not merely a quality-of-life factor but a cancer and longevity risk variable. While this is mouse research, the MDM2 pathway finding is specific enough to inform human research priorities.

Second-order effect: The study raises the prospect of autologous FMT — preserving one's own young microbiome for later restoration — as a preventive strategy. This is likely 5–10 years from clinical practice, but it is precisely the kind of early science your organisation can track and contextualise for a senior audience now, ahead of the commercial noise that will follow.

3. Protein + Resistance Exercise Combination: Precise Dosing Parameters Now Defined for Sarcopenia

Source: MDPI Biomolecules — Synergistic Effects of Protein Intake and Exercise on Biomarkers of Sarcopenia: A Systematic Review (January 2026)

What happened: A systematic review published in January 2026 synthesised 7 RCTs (260 participants total) examining the combination of resistance exercise and protein supplementation in older adults with sarcopenia. The review found that three sessions per week of resistance training at 60–80% of one-repetition maximum, combined with at least 15 g/day of protein supplementation, produced synergistic improvements across both functional and biochemical markers. Specifically: myostatin, activin, and IL-6 were reduced (anti-catabolic effect); IGF-1, follistatin, and IL-10 were increased (anabolic and anti-inflammatory effects); and gains in muscle strength, fat-free mass, and muscle fibre cross-sectional area were observed. Protein supplementation alone produced modest strength gains but minimal effects on physical performance.

Why this matters to you: Until now, guidance on protein-exercise combinations for sarcopenia has been broadly framed (e.g., "increase protein intake and exercise more"). This review is among the first to establish specific operational thresholds that educators and practitioners can translate directly into advice for older adults: 15 g/day minimum supplementary protein and 3×/week resistance at ≥60% 1RM. These numbers are actionable and communicable to a non-specialist 75+ audience.

Second-order effect: The biomarker panel identified — myostatin, follistatin, IGF-1, IL-6, IL-10 — is a plausible monitoring framework for future wearable or blood-test integration. Organisations that can explain these markers to older adults and their carers will be positioned as the translation layer between clinical science and personal health practice.
