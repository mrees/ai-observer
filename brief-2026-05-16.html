Key Developments

1. Restoring a Youthful Gut Microbiome Reversed Liver Ageing and Prevented Liver Cancer in Mice

Source: ScienceDaily — DDW 2026 Presentation (9 May 2026) / Nature World News (15 May 2026)
What happened:

Research presented at Digestive Disease Week 2026 found that transplanting a mouse's own preserved youthful gut microbiome back into the same animal at older age reversed multiple hallmarks of liver ageing — including inflammation, fibrosis, mitochondrial decline, and DNA damage — and prevented liver cancer entirely. No tumours developed in transplanted mice, whereas hepatocellular carcinoma appeared in control animals. Critically, the study used each animal's own preserved microbiome rather than a donor's, minimising immune rejection and infection risk. The research team intends to begin human trials in the near future.

Why this matters to you:

The gut–liver axis is one of the most clinically significant pathways in ageing research, and this study provides the most direct causal evidence to date that microbiome composition — not just genetics or liver disease — drives hepatic ageing trajectories. For your 75+ audience, liver health is a silent concern: age-related liver dysfunction is strongly linked to metabolic disease, medication processing, and nutritional absorption. This finding strengthens the case for dietary intervention as a practical route to microbiome preservation — a message you can deliver clearly and credibly now, ahead of the commercial sector oversimplifying it.

Second-order effect:

This study provides the mechanistic rationale for why dietary patterns that support microbiome diversity in mid-life may have compounding benefits that only become visible in the 75+ age group — making the case for earlier, sustained dietary intervention rather than reactive management in old age.

2. Sarcopenia Combined with Frailty More Than Doubles Cardiovascular Disease Risk — Meta-Analysis of 2.3 Million Participants

Source: Journal of Global Health — Combined effect of frailty and sarcopenia on CVD risk (2026) / Journal of the American Heart Association (2026)
What happened:

A large-scale meta-analysis published in 2026 drawing on data from approximately 2.3 million participants found that sarcopenia alone, frailty alone, and their co-occurrence each independently and progressively increase cardiovascular disease risk. Individuals with both conditions carried more than double the CVD risk compared to robust non-sarcopenic adults, with a hazard ratio of 3.29 for those who remained persistently frail over time. Frailty statistically mediated approximately 35% of the association between sarcopenia and CVD, confirming that these conditions share biological pathways — including chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction — rather than simply co-occurring by chance.

Why this matters to you:

A 2.3-million-participant meta-analysis gives the sarcopenia–cardiovascular disease link the same statistical weight as major cardiovascular risk factor evidence. For a 75+ population where both conditions are common, this means sarcopenia is no longer just a mobility and falls concern — it is a cardiovascular risk factor requiring nutritional and exercise intervention. This finding directly supports your advocacy for routine muscle mass assessment and protein-adequate dietary guidance in older adults, and gives your educational content a powerful clinical anchor.

Second-order effect:

As cardiologists begin to treat sarcopenia as a modifiable cardiovascular risk factor, demand for nutritional interventions — specifically protein supplementation and resistance training guidance — in cardiac care settings will grow. This positions nutrition science professionals with expertise in the 75+ population at the intersection of geriatric and cardiovascular medicine.

3. Elicit Now Supports PRISMA 2020 — AI-Assisted Systematic Reviews Are Now Reproducible and Auditable

Source: Elicit — Systematic Review (updated 6 May 2026) / AI Tool Ranked — Elicit Review 2026
What happened:

As of 6 May 2026, Elicit's systematic review tool now supports PRISMA 2020 guidelines and has been redesigned to be reproducible, traceable, and auditable at every step of the review process. Elicit searches across 138 million papers, extracts data into structured tables, and generates automated reports. Independent evaluations report 95% search recall and 97–99% screening accuracy. The platform costs from free to $49/month for professional use. A separate independent evaluation published in JMIR AI found that Elicit achieved 81.4% data extraction accuracy compared to 86.7% for human reviewers — a gap that was not statistically significant.

Why this matters to you:

PRISMA 2020 compliance means that systematic reviews produced with Elicit can now meet the minimum reporting standards required for publication and policy submission. For a non-profit nutrition science organisation producing evidence-based educational content, this reduces the resource cost of conducting rigorous literature reviews substantially. The 80% time saving reported by users is credible given the platform's capability profile. The accuracy parity with human reviewers at the screening stage means the tool is appropriate for initial evidence synthesis, with human expert review focused on interpretation rather than retrieval.

Second-order effect:

PRISMA-compliant AI systematic review tools lower the barrier for smaller organisations to produce publication-quality evidence summaries — including in nutrition science. This will increase the volume of AI-assisted reviews entering peer-reviewed literature, making it progressively more important for readers and educators to understand whether a review was AI-assisted and how to evaluate its quality accordingly.

4. Real-World Study: Mobile Health App Achieves 95% Medication Adherence in Adults Aged 65 and Over

Source: Frontiers in Digital Health — Mobile health apps for older adults: real-world evidence (2026)
What happened:

A real-world evidence study published in Frontiers in Digital Health in 2026 examined engagement and medication adherence among 250 adults aged 65 and over (mean age 70.1 years) using the Perx Health mobile app. Median medication adherence reached 95.0%, verified via mobile Directly Observed Therapy — participants uploaded photos of themselves taking medications. The app used games, rewards, and social recognition to support adherence. Over 61% of participants were female and 61.6% were Australia-based. The study is notable for using objective verification methods rather than self-report alone, lending its adherence figures greater credibility than most app-based studies.

Why this matters to you:

Polypharmacy and poor medication adherence are major contributors to preventable hospitalisation in the 75+ population, and most digital health tools are designed and tested in younger cohorts. A real-world 95% adherence rate in a predominantly female, older adult population using a gamified app is a strong signal that well-designed mobile health tools can achieve meaningful behaviour change in your target demographic — extending the case for digital health literacy as a practical health intervention for seniors, not merely a technology aspiration.

Second-order effect:

The gamification and social recognition elements that drove adherence in this study are design principles equally applicable to nutrition behaviour change tools — meal tracking, hydration reminders, and supplement regimens. The evidence base for this approach in older adults is now stronger, providing useful design guidance for any digital nutrition tool developed for the 75+ population.

5. Protein Intake of 1.2 g/kg/day Outperforms Standard Recommendations for Preserving Muscle Mass in Sarcopenic Elderly Women

Source: PMC / Frontiers in Nutrition — Protein intake and muscle mass in elderly females with sarcopenia (2026)
What happened:

A randomised trial of 126 participants aged 60–75 years compared protein intakes of 0.8 g/kg body weight per day (the current standard recommendation) with 1.2 g/kg per day in elderly women with confirmed sarcopenia. The higher intake group showed significant gains in muscle strength and cross-sectional muscle area — particularly in the thigh and calf — alongside reductions in waist circumference and fat mass. MRI, handgrip, and knee flexion tests confirmed the differences. The study adds to a growing body of evidence that current dietary protein recommendations for older adults, set at 0.8 g/kg, are insufficient to prevent or reverse sarcopenia.

Why this matters to you:

The gap between the standard 0.8 g/kg recommendation and the 1.2 g/kg level shown to preserve muscle mass in this study is nutritionally and practically significant. For your 75+ female audience — who are at elevated sarcopenia risk and frequently consuming well below even the standard recommendation — this finding strengthens the case for explicit, accessible guidance on protein distribution across daily meals. This is a directly actionable educational message that does not require pharmaceutical intervention or medical supervision.

Second-order effect:

As evidence accumulates that 0.8 g/kg is inadequate for older adults, pressure on national dietary guidelines to revise upward will grow. Organisations that have already been communicating higher protein targets to their 75+ audiences will be ahead of the official guidance — a credibility advantage when guidelines eventually shift.
