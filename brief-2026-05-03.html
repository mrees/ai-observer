Key Developments

1. MIND Diet Shown to Slow Structural Brain Ageing by 2.5 Years

Source: BMJ Group / Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery & Psychiatry (Loughborough University press release, March 2026)

What happened: A prospective cohort study of over 1,600 adults from the Framingham Heart Study offspring cohort found that every three-point increase in MIND diet adherence corresponded to 20% less grey matter shrinkage over 12 years — equivalent to a 2.5-year delay in structural brain ageing. Berries and poultry emerged as the specific components most strongly linked to slower ventricle enlargement and preserved grey matter volume. Participants were followed from an average age of 60, making this one of the longer dietary imaging studies of its kind.

Why this matters to you: This is the clearest structural brain-imaging evidence yet linking a named dietary pattern to measurable slowing of brain ageing. For a nutrition educator targeting adults 75+, this gives you specific, communicable foods (berries, poultry) rather than abstract pattern advice — directly usable in educational content.

Second-order effect: As structural brain MRI data becomes cheaper and more available through digital health platforms, dietary interventions linked to imaging biomarkers (not just cognitive tests) will become a new standard of evidence — reshaping how funders and policymakers evaluate nutrition programmes.

2. TEAMS RCT: Protein Supplementation Helps Frail Older Adults — But Only Those Already Under-Eating

Source: PMC / BMC Geriatrics (TEAMS Study, results published May 2026)

What happened: A 12-week RCT randomised 295 frail community-dwelling older adults into progressive resistance training (PRT) alone or PRT combined with a protein intervention. Overall, adding protein did not significantly improve muscle strength, mass, or physical performance. However, exploratory analyses showed that participants consuming below 0.8 g/kg/day at baseline experienced approximately 10% greater gains in leg press and leg extension strength when protein was added — the most underfed subgroup showed the largest benefit.

Why this matters to you: This sharpens earlier signals considerably. The question for your 75+ audience is not "does protein help?" but "are they already meeting minimum threshold?" The practical implication is that habitual protein intake screening should precede any supplementation recommendation — a more precise message for community nutrition education.

Second-order effect: This finding strengthens the case for personalised nutrition thresholds over population-level RDA guidance — an argument Michael's organisation can make directly to policymakers reviewing the current 0.8 g/kg/day RDA.

3. NIH All of Us Wearables Dataset Published: 59,000 Participants, 14 Years of Fitbit Data

Source: Nature Medicine (All of Us Research Program, 2026)

What happened: The NIH All of Us Research Program has published a characterisation of its wearables dataset in Nature Medicine: over 59,000 participants, 14 years of Fitbit data, 39 million step observations and 31 million sleep observations. Critically, nearly half of participants also contributed electronic health records, genomic data, and physical measurements, making it one of the most deeply linked longitudinal digital health datasets ever assembled. The dataset explicitly prioritises populations historically underrepresented in research.

Why this matters to you: This is the infrastructure layer that researchers — including those in nutrition and ageing — can now build on. Linking step counts, sleep patterns, and dietary data to clinical outcomes across age groups is now feasible at scale, with publicly accessible data for qualified researchers.

Second-order effect: Organisations without their own data-collection capacity can now piggyback on All of Us to test hypotheses about physical activity, sleep, and dietary behaviour in older adults — lowering the barrier to producing peer-reviewed evidence considerably.

4. EU AI Act Implementation Tools Due Q2 2026 — High-Risk Medical AI Rules Apply from August

Source: Wilson Sonsini / EU AI Act regulatory timeline (2026)

What happened: The European Commission is publishing support instruments and implementation guidance for the EU AI Act in Q2 2026, ahead of the August 2026 date when high-risk AI obligations formally apply to medical devices and in vitro diagnostics. Full compliance for all high-risk AI in healthcare is required by August 2027. In parallel, the FDA's January 2026 guidance substantially reduced oversight of low-risk AI health tools, widening the regulatory divergence between the US and EU approaches to health AI.

Why this matters to you: Any digital health tool targeting older adults — from dietary tracking apps to wearable frailty monitors — will need to be classified under either or both regulatory frameworks if operating across jurisdictions. The EU–US divergence creates real compliance uncertainty for developers and deployers.

Second-order effect: Organisations that publish practical guidance on which AI tools are safe and validated for use in research with older adults will become important reference points for an audience of researchers trying to navigate this complexity.

5. Longevity Sector Funding Solidifies: Consumer Platforms Now Rival Drug Pipelines

Source: New Market Pitch / Longevity Startup Funding Analysis 2025–2026

What happened: Between May 2025 and April 2026, pure-play longevity companies raised an average round of $56.6M (median $27.3M). Consumer-facing longevity platforms — diagnostics, wearables, and supplements — collectively raised over $700M in the period, signalling that investor confidence has shifted beyond drug pipelines. Notable: Loyal, focused on canine longevity biology (a validated model for human ageing research), closed a $100M Series C in Q1 2026.

Why this matters to you: The separation of longevity funding into drug pipelines vs. consumer platforms is significant: the consumer layer is where older adults will actually encounter these products, yet evidence standards there remain far weaker than for drug development. This is a gap Michael's educational mission is well placed to address.

Second-order effect: The scale of capital entering consumer longevity products will generate considerable marketing noise directed at the 75+ demographic. Independent, evidence-based educational organisations become more — not less — necessary as this market grows.

Signals Worth Watching

  • Gut microbiome and physical activity correlation: A study published in Frontiers in Aging (April 2026) found positive associations between Prevotella copri and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity in older adults, with Roseburia species linked to better mobility and handgrip strength — consistent with earlier signals but now with larger independent replication.
  • JMIR phenomenological study on wearables: A 2026 study of older adults using wearables with real-time feedback found that meaningful engagement requires adaptive design respecting users' authentic movement practices — not just technical sophistication. Usability, not sensor quality, determines uptake in this population.
  • Carbohydrate type and dementia risk: A January 2026 ScienceDaily item (citing a peer-reviewed study) found that low-glycaemic-index foods — fruit, legumes, whole grains — may reduce dementia risk independently of overall diet quality. Watch for full publication details.
  • PROMIS questionnaire for sarcopenia screening: Self-completed PROMIS Physical Function and Mobility domains independently predict sarcopenia risk in community-dwelling older adults — opening the door to low-cost digital screening at scale (Scientific Reports, 2025).
  • Stanford Longevity Pilot Grants 2025–2026: Stanford Medicine's longevity and healthy ageing pilot grant programme is active this cycle. Worth monitoring for emerging research priorities that signal where the field's next questions lie.

The 3 Things to Think About Today

  1. Translate the MIND diet evidence into your educational content now. The Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery & Psychiatry study is the strongest structural brain-imaging evidence to date for a dietary pattern in ageing. Unlike many nutrition findings, it names specific foods (berries, poultry) with quantified effect sizes. This is unusually communicable to a lay 75+ audience and directly supports your core mission. Produce accessible materials while the study has public attention.
  2. Reframe your protein messaging around intake thresholds, not supplementation. The TEAMS RCT result confirms that protein matters most for those already falling below 0.8 g/kg/day — which is a large proportion of adults 75+. The actionable insight is to help seniors understand and check their own intake level before any supplement decision. This is a more precise and defensible educational message than generic protein promotion.
  3. Position your organisation as a trusted navigator in an increasingly noisy longevity market. With $700M+ flowing into consumer longevity platforms and the EU AI Act creating compliance uncertainty, the 75+ demographic will face a growing volume of poorly evidenced product claims alongside genuinely useful tools. An organisation that curates and translates evidence — rather than promotes products — has a structural advantage in this environment. Consider making that positioning explicit in your communications.
